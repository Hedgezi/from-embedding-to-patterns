\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Informal group chats have become a primary medium of everyday communication, where users frequently share short messages, jokes, and recurring expressions. Within such conversations, certain phrases or semantic patterns reappear over time and may temporarily dominate discussion. Detecting these recurring patterns is challenging due to the noisy, unstructured, and highly dynamic nature of chat data. \\
Traditional clustering methods are not well suited for this setting. Chat messages are typically short, diverse, and often include slight variations of the same expression, making it difficult to group them using simple lexical or syntactic similarity. At the same time, the number and structure of recurring patterns are unknown in advance, which limits the applicability of methods that require predefined cluster counts. \\
In this work, we approach this problem from an unsupervised perspective using sentence embeddings and clustering methods. We hypothesize that recurring patterns in chat data correspond to dense regions in the embedding space, where semantically similar messages appear frequently. Under this assumption, density-based clustering methods are particularly suitable, as they can capture clusters of arbitrary shape and identify noise. \\
We systematically evaluate several clustering approaches, including K-Means as a baseline, HDBSCAN and DBSCAN as density-based methods, and BERTopic as a higher-level pipeline combining dimensionality reduction, clustering, and topic representation. We also investigate the role of dimensionality reduction using UMAP in improving clustering performance in high-dimensional embedding spaces. \\
Our experiments on a real-world chat dataset show that standard clustering approaches either produce overly coarse groupings or fail to capture a significant portion of the data. In contrast, density-based methods combined with dimensionality reduction are better suited for identifying locally dense semantic patterns, although challenges such as cluster fragmentation remain. \\
The main contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate the problem of detecting recurring patterns in informal chat data as an unsupervised clustering task.
    \item We propose a density-based interpretation of such patterns as compact regions in embedding space.
    \item We provide a comparative analysis of multiple clustering pipelines on real-world chat data.
    \item We analyze the impact of dimensionality reduction and clustering parameters on the structure and interpretability of the resulting clusters.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The dataset consists of messages extracted from a private Telegram group chat of approximately 17 participants over a period of 6 months. The messages are in Russian. \\
The initial dataset contains 42,968 messages. After filtering out non-text content (images, videos, audio) and short messages (less than 5 characters), 32,738 text messages remain. Short messages were removed due to their limited semantic content for embedding-based analysis.

\subsection{Embeddings}

Sentence embeddings were computed using the \texttt{sentence-transformers} library with the \texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2} model. This model was selected due to its strong performance in multilingual semantic similarity tasks and its support for the Russian language.
The model produces dense vector representations of sentences, allowing a semantic similarity comparison between messages, which is essential for clustering semantically related content.

\subsection{Clustering Methods}

Several clustering approaches are considered. As a baseline, K-Means is used due to its simplicity and widespread adoption.
In addition, density-based clustering methods are explored, including HDBSCAN and DBSCAN. These methods are particularly suitable for detecting clusters of arbitrary shape and handling noise, which is expected in informal chat data.
HDBSCAN is used as a hierarchical density-based approach requiring minimal parameter tuning, while DBSCAN is evaluated as a more controllable alternative with explicit density thresholds.

\subsection{Patterns}

In this work, a “meme” or “trend” is defined as a recurring semantic pattern in messages, which may manifest as repeated phrases, paraphrased expressions, or variations of a common concept.
Such patterns may appear as exact repetitions (\eg, copied text) or as semantically similar variations with minor lexical differences.
For example, references to a specific entity may vary slightly in wording (\eg, synonymous or closely related expressions), while still representing the same underlying concept.
The objective of clustering is to group such semantically related messages into coherent clusters, even in the presence of noise and linguistic variation.

\subsection{Pipeline}

The prepared pipeline consists of the following steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item text preprocessing and filtering,
    \item embedding computation,
    \item clustering of embeddings,
    \item analysis of resulting clusters.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All experiments are conducted on the dataset described in Section~\ref{sec:data}, consisting of 32,738 Russian-language text messages extracted from a private group chat. \\
Sentence embeddings are computed using the \texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2} model from the \texttt{sentence-transformers} library. \\
We will evaluate several clustering approaches: \\
(i) K-Means as a baseline, \\
(ii) HDBSCAN on raw embeddings, \\
(iii) UMAP followed by HDBSCAN, \\
(iv) DBSCAN as a density-based alternative with explicit control over parameters, \\
and (v) BERTopic as a higher-level topic modeling pipeline. \\ \\
For K-Means, multiple values of $K$ are explored to analyze the sensitivity of clustering results. \\  
For HDBSCAN and DBSCAN, parameters controlling minimum cluster size and density thresholds are varied to study their effect on cluster formation. \\
In the UMAP-based setup, dimensionality reduction is applied prior to clustering using default parameters of BERTopic library unless stated otherwise. \\
To ensure comparability, all methods use the same embedding representations.

\subsection{Baseline Analysis}

As a baseline, we first evaluate the K-Means clustering on message embeddings. K-Means is a natural baseline due to its simplicity and widespread use, but its application in this setting raises an immediate difficulty: the number of clusters $K$ must be specified in advance, while the true number of recurring themes or meme-like patterns in the chat is unknown. \\
For $K = 1000$, the expected average cluster size in a uniform partition would be approximately 33 messages, which is close to the mean cluster size observed. In this regime, clusters are often too small and fragmented to be interpreted reliably. Although some semantically similar messages are grouped together, clusters may still contain messages with noticeably different meanings, making interpretation difficult. \\
For $K = 100$, the mean cluster size increases to approximately 327 messages, and some clusters become more interpretable. For example, one cluster primarily contains words referring to relative days (e.g., \textit{yesterday}, \textit{today}, \textit{tomorrow}), while another is centered around references to a specific person, Ivan. However, such clusters tend to reflect broad semantic themes rather than localized recurring patterns of interest. \\
These observations suggest that K-Means is highly sensitive to the choice of $K$ and may struggle to capture dense, irregular, and locally concentrated semantic patterns in informal chat data. This motivates the use of density-based clustering methods in the following experiments.

\subsection{HDBSCAN on Raw Embeddings}

We next evaluate HDBSCAN directly on the raw sentence embeddings in order to test whether density-based clustering alone is sufficient to recover recurring semantic patterns in the chat. \\
Using the default configuration (minimum cluster size = 5, cluster selection epsilon = 0), HDBSCAN labels 28,631 out of 32,738 messages as noise. Among the detected clusters, the median cluster size is 7. These results suggest that the method is highly conservative in the original embedding space and captures only a small fraction of the dataset in cluster form. \\
Qualitative inspection shows that many clusters contain genuinely similar messages. At the same time, the clustering is often overly fragmented: repeated expressions and minor spelling variations with nearly identical meaning are not always assigned to the same cluster. For example, \textit{ahahah} and \textit{ahaha} frequently appear in different clusters despite their obvious similarity in conversational use. \\
This behavior suggests that raw embedding space may be too sparse for reliable density estimation in this task. Since the embedding vectors are 384-dimensional, dimensionality reduction appears to be a natural next step. Therefore, the following experiment applies UMAP before HDBSCAN in order to obtain a more compact representation of local semantic neighborhoods.

\subsection{Effect of Dimensionality Reduction}

In this experiment, we evaluate whether dimensionality reduction with UMAP improves HDBSCAN clustering compared to clustering on raw embeddings. The motivation is to make the local structure of the embedding space more suitable for density-based clustering.
We use the standard UMAP configuration adopted in the BERTopic library: \texttt{n\_neighbors=15}, \texttt{n\_components=5}, \texttt{min\_dist=0.0}, and \texttt{metric='cosine'}. This setup is also convenient for later comparison with BERTopic as an integrated pipeline.
After applying UMAP before HDBSCAN, the number of messages labeled as noise decreases to 11,318 out of 32,738. At the same time, the method produces 1,348 clusters. Compared to HDBSCAN on raw embeddings, this indicates a substantial improvement in coverage, since a much larger fraction of the dataset is assigned to clusters. However, the resulting number of clusters remains high, which suggests that the clustering is still relatively fragmented.
A qualitative inspection of the clusters shows that many of them correspond to repeated phrases or recurring catchphrases (e.g., \textit{студия красоты}, \textit{фо вотово}), while others are organized around narrow semantic topics, such as smoking or cigarettes. This suggests that dimensionality reduction improves the ability of HDBSCAN to recover locally dense semantic patterns and repeated conversational expressions.
At the same time, the method still does not cleanly separate recurring phrase-based patterns from broader thematic groupings. These observations motivate two further directions: evaluating BERTopic as a higher-level pipeline built on the same components, and testing DBSCAN with explicit control over the neighborhood radius under the hypothesis that repeated catchphrases form tighter clusters than broader topic-based groups.

\subsection{BERTopic}

We next evaluate BERTopic as a higher-level pipeline built on top of UMAP and HDBSCAN, with the additional advantage of automatic topic representation and labeling. The goal of this experiment is to determine whether such an integrated framework improves the interpretability or quality of the discovered clusters compared to the manually constructed UMAP + HDBSCAN setup.
For this experiment, we used the default HDBSCAN settings together with the standard UMAP configuration adopted by BERTopic. The resulting clustering structure is only slightly different from the previous setup. In particular, the median cluster size increases from 10 to 11, while the number of clusters increases from 1,348 to 1,417. These differences are small and may be explained by stochastic components or pipeline implementation details.
Overall, BERTopic does not substantially change the clustering behavior compared to the direct UMAP + HDBSCAN configuration. Its main advantage lies in the convenience of an integrated workflow and automatic topic labeling, rather than in a clearer recovery of the specific recurring patterns of interest. In particular, the number of clusters remains too high to isolate the narrow meme-like trends and repeated catchphrases that motivate this work.
This suggests that the overall pipeline still groups many semantically narrow but distinct patterns separately. To address this, the next experiment evaluates DBSCAN with explicit control over the neighborhood radius. Under the hypothesis that repeated catchphrases occupy very tight regions in the embedding space, a small $\epsilon$ may help recover more specific and focused patterns.

\subsection{DBSCAN as a More Controllable Alternative}

We next evaluate DBSCAN as a more controllable alternative to HDBSCAN by explicitly varying the neighborhood radius $\epsilon$. The goal of this experiment is to test the hypothesis that repeated catchphrases and meme-like patterns occupy tighter regions in embedding space than broader thematic groups. \\
We first consider $\epsilon = 0.01$. In this setting, the number of clusters decreases from 1,417 (in BERTopic) to 954, while the median cluster size drops to 5. This suggests that the clustering shifts toward more localized and specific patterns rather than broad semantic themes, although some fragmentation remains. A manual inspection of representative messages from the clusters shows that many of them correspond to recurring expressions and conversational patterns of interest. Roughly 10--15\% of the clusters appear to represent such patterns. \\
For $\epsilon = 0.005$, the number of clusters decreases further to 602, and the median cluster size becomes 4, only slightly above the minimum cluster size. Based on manual inspection, approximately 18\% of the clusters correspond to repeated patterns of interest. The remaining clusters mostly fall into two categories. First, some clusters capture highly specific discussion topics from the chat and therefore provide useful insight into conversational structure. Second, other clusters correspond to common conversational phrases such as ``What's up, guys?'' or ``That's not true.'' Although these are not the main target of this work, they still reflect recurring linguistic behavior in the chat. \\
Finally, for $\epsilon = 0.001$, the neighborhood radius becomes too restrictive. In this case, only 175 clusters remain, with a median cluster size of 5. Most of these clusters correspond to very common short expressions sent as standalone messages, such as laughter or brief emotional reactions. While these clusters are highly compact, they are often too trivial to capture the broader recurring patterns of interest. \\

Overall, these results suggest that DBSCAN provides more direct control over the granularity of the clustering than HDBSCAN. Moderate values of $\epsilon$ offer the best trade-off between specificity and interpretability: larger values still allow broader topic-based groupings, while very small values isolate only trivial repeated expressions. This supports the hypothesis that recurring catchphrases form tight local neighborhoods in embedding space, but that overly restrictive density thresholds may over-fragment the data.
